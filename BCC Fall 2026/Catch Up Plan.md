## 2. Hondo business case analysis draft

### 1. Who are the primary stakeholders?

The primary stakeholders are George, his family, the employees at the Hondo plant and their families, the residents and businesses of Hondo, Ardnak Plastics and its owners, Bill and the company’s other managers, the company’s customers, the EPA, and the people who live on both sides of the U.S.–Mexico border. Mexican workers who might receive jobs if the plant moves are also stakeholders. Finally, the natural environment and future residents are affected because air pollution can cause long-term harm even when that harm is not immediately visible.

### 2. What are the ethical issues?

The central ethical issue is whether Ardnak should protect people and obey environmental standards even when compliance reduces short-term profits. Scheduling heavy emissions at night would deliberately deceive regulators while increasing pollution, so it would be both dishonest and harmful. Moving the plant to Mexico only to avoid U.S. environmental rules would not solve the pollution problem; it would transfer risk to a place with weaker enforcement while the contaminated air could still cross the border. At the same time, closing or moving the plant would threaten hundreds of jobs and damage the Hondo community. George is therefore being pressured to choose between economic security, legal compliance, public health, and loyalty to his employer.

### 3. What are the possible alternatives?

Ardnak could install the required smokestack scrubbers immediately. If the company cannot pay the full cost at once, it could seek financing, environmental grants or tax incentives, postpone less essential spending, or phase the installation over a short and enforceable schedule. The company could ask the EPA for a formal compliance plan while immediately reducing production during the most polluting processes. It could also hire an independent environmental engineer to identify less expensive changes to materials, equipment, or production methods. Ardnak should communicate honestly with employees and the community and investigate whether temporary cost reductions can preserve jobs during the upgrade. The unethical alternatives are hiding emissions at night or relocating solely to escape regulation.

### 4. Which alternative provides the greatest net benefit to the greatest number?

The strongest alternative is a transparent, phased compliance plan that begins reducing emissions immediately and finances permanent scrubbers as quickly as possible. This protects the health of residents and workers, keeps the plant and its jobs in Hondo, reduces future fines and legal risk, and preserves Ardnak’s reputation. The company may accept lower short-term profits, but the long-term benefit is greater because it avoids continued harm, repeated penalties, and the possibility of a forced shutdown.

### 5. What would you choose if you were George, and why?

If I were George, I would refuse to manipulate the production schedule to mislead the EPA. I would document the violations and present Bill with a written compliance proposal that includes immediate emission reductions, an engineering review, financing options, and a deadline for installing scrubbers. I would also request a formal meeting with senior management and the EPA so that the company could negotiate a lawful plan without abandoning the community. If the company still demanded deception or continued illegal pollution, I would document that pressure and seek guidance from the appropriate compliance or legal authority. This choice is not the easiest in the short term, but it protects the most stakeholders and treats environmental responsibility as a real cost of doing business rather than a problem to hide or export.

- Business: an organization that provides goods or services while seeking to satisfy customers; a for-profit business also seeks profit.

- Entrepreneur: a person who accepts the risks of organizing and operating a business.
- Risk–reward relationship: potential returns generally rise with the amount of risk accepted.
- Stakeholders: customers, employees, investors, suppliers, communities, governments, and others affected by the business.
- Factors of production: land/natural resources, labor, capital, entrepreneurship, and knowledge.
- Business environment: economic/legal, technological, competitive, social, and global forces.

- Economics, macroeconomics, and microeconomics.
- Supply, demand, equilibrium price, shortages, and surpluses.
- Capitalism, socialism, communism, and mixed economies.
- Private property, freedom of choice, profit motive, and competition.
- Market structures: perfect competition, monopolistic competition, oligopoly, and monopoly.
- Gross domestic product, unemployment rate, inflation, deflation, and the consumer price index.
- Business-cycle stages: expansion, peak, contraction/recession, and trough/recovery.
- Fiscal policy versus monetary policy.
- How productivity and innovation affect economic growth and standards of living.
- Four types of unemployment: frictional, structural, cyclical, and seasonal.
- Demand-pull inflation versus cost-push inflation.
- Consumer price index versus producer price index.

- Imports, exports, balance of trade, and balance of payments.
- Absolute advantage versus comparative advantage.
- Why countries trade and specialize.
- Exchange-rate effects on import and export prices.
- Trade barriers: tariffs, quotas, embargoes, and non-tariff barriers.
- Free trade and protectionism.
- Entry strategies: exporting, licensing, franchising, contract manufacturing, joint ventures, strategic alliances, and foreign direct investment.

Review:

- Sole proprietorship: easy formation and control, but unlimited personal liability.
- General partnership: shared resources and management, but every general partner can have unlimited liability for business obligations.
- Limited partnership: at least one general partner manages and has unlimited liability; limited partners generally invest without managing and risk only their investment.
- Corporation: separate state-chartered legal entity, limited shareholder liability, transferable ownership, unlimited life, more regulation, and possible double taxation for a C corporation.
- S corporation: organized like a corporation but generally taxed through its shareholders.
- Limited liability company: limited liability with partnership-style tax flexibility.
- Franchise: right to use an established brand and operating system in exchange for fees and restrictions.
- Horizontal, vertical, and conglomerate mergers; acquisition and leveraged buyout.
- Cooperative and joint-venture structures.
- Entrepreneurial traits, opportunity recognition, and calculated risk.
- Entrepreneur types: micropreneur, growth-oriented entrepreneur, multipreneur, and intrapreneur.
- Business-plan components: company idea, owner qualifications, product/service, market analysis, implementation/operations, and financial plan.
- Common reasons small businesses succeed or fail.
- Debt versus equity financing; angel investors, venture capital, and Small Business Investment Companies.
- Role of the Small Business Administration: loan guarantees, management assistance, consulting, courses, publications, and access to federal contracting opportunities.
- Small-business advantages: flexibility, personal service, efficiency, and ability to serve niches.
- Small-business challenges: limited capital and managerial depth, regulatory burden, and the owner’s large time commitment.

### Draft Part 2 reflection

> Mimetic desire describes how people often learn what to want by observing other people who become models for them. This influence can be conscious, such as following a professional whose career I respect, or unconscious, such as treating popularity as evidence that something is valuable. Positive social-media models can use this mechanism to make constructive behavior visible and socially desirable. Posts that show people contributing knowledge, cleaning public spaces, teaching others, or preserving cultural material can encourage viewers to imitate the behavior because they see participation, recognition, and belonging attached to it.
>
> One desire in my own life is to build a stronger creative and technical portfolio. That desire has been shaped by artists, designers, developers, and students who share finished projects as well as their process. Their examples make the goal feel achievable, but they can also create pressure to compare polished outcomes. Thinking about mimetic desire helps me choose models based on useful habits—consistent practice, curiosity, and sharing work honestly—instead of copying surface-level success.
