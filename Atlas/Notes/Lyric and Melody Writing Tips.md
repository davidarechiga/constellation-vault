# Lyric and Melody Writing Tips

Dave's working guide to writing lyrics and melodies. It's written with the new EP in mind ([[Post-Hardcore EP Production Blueprint]]), but the habits apply to any Sprawling song. Old lyric drafts live in the archive: [[📝 Song Lyrics & Music Writing]]. Nothing here writes the lyrics for you; it's a set of tools and a checklist.

## The core idea

A song is **one feeling, one image, one hook**. Everything else supports those three. When a song stalls, it's usually because it has two feelings fighting or no clear hook.

## Part 1: Melody

### Start with the rhythm of the words

- Sing or speak the line **on one note** first. Find where the natural stresses fall, then put the important syllables on strong beats (1 and 3 in 4/4).
- Melody that fights the natural stress of the words sounds awkward. If a line feels wrong, change the words before you change the notes.
- Hum nonsense syllables ("la," "da") over the chords and record it. Keep the shapes you like and fit words to them later.

### Shape and contour

- **Verses sit low and move in small steps.** Narrow range (about a 5th), lots of repeated notes, so the lyric carries the song.
- **Choruses sit higher and open up.** Land the highest or longest note of the song on the hook word. Leaps (a 4th, 5th or octave) feel like release because the verse was stepwise.
- **Contour = the line's outline.** An arch (up then down) feels settled. A climb that doesn't come down feels like tension. A fall feels like resignation. Choose on purpose.
- **Stepwise motion plus the occasional leap.** After a big leap, the melody usually steps back the other way. It sounds natural.

### Use the chord tones and the tension notes

- **Strong beats on chord tones** (root, 3rd, 5th) sound stable. **Non-chord tones** (the 2nd, 4th, 6th, 7th) sound unsettled, so use them on weak beats or as a passing note.
- A held non-chord tone that resolves a beat later is the "ache" in a lot of emo and post-hardcore. For example, hold the 2nd over a `sus2` chord and let it fall to the root or 3rd. Your open-drone voicings in the blueprint are perfect for this.
- **Pentatonic first.** If you freeze, write the melody on the minor or major pentatonic, then add one or two outside notes for color.

### Repetition and variation

- **The hook repeats.** A melody the listener can sing after one chorus beats a clever one they can't.
- Repeat a phrase, then **change the end of it** (call and response). It feels like a conversation.
- **Don't repeat a section exactly a third time.** Add a new note, a lift in range, or drop an instrument.
- Write the **first phrase of the chorus** as a short, singable motif (3 to 5 notes). Build from it.

### Harmony and doubles

- Add harmonies **a 3rd above or below**, or an **octave or 5th** for the gang vocals and strained choruses. Keep the lead's contour; harmonies follow it.
- A harmony that crosses the lead sounds muddy. Keep it clearly above or below.

### Match melody to section energy

| Section | Range | Rhythm | Feel |
|---------|-------|--------|------|
| Verse | Narrow, low | Conversational, repeated notes | Intimate |
| Pre-chorus | Climbs | Shorter phrases, more notes per bar | Tension |
| Chorus | Wide, high | Longer notes, space | Release |
| Bridge | Different from both | New rhythm or a spoken delivery | Surprise, doubt |

## Part 2: Lyrics

### Before you write

- Write **one sentence** of what the song is about. If it needs "and," it's two songs.
- Pick **one image or object** (a room, a car, a phone, a coat) and keep returning to it.
- Decide **who is speaking to whom.** "You" songs feel urgent; "I" songs feel confessional. Don't drift between them without a reason.

### Write the chorus first

- The chorus is the **thesis**: one clear line that could be shouted by a crowd. It should work on its own, without the verses.
- Keep it **short and plain.** Plain words, strongly felt, beat ornate language in this genre.
- The **title** is usually the hook line. If the title appears in the chorus, people remember the song.

### Verses

- Verses are **concrete and small**: specific details (a place, a thing someone said, a time of day), not abstract feelings.
- **Show, don't explain.** "The porch light's still on" says more than "I'm lonely."
- Each verse adds **new information**. Verse 2 should move the story, not repeat verse 1 in different words.
- Keep verse lines to **about 4 lines per stanza** so the melody can breathe.

### The bridge

- The bridge **changes the meaning** of something. Reuse a line from verse 1 with a new feeling, or say the thing the song has been avoiding.
- Make it sound different: lower, quieter, a different rhythm, or half-spoken.

### Rhyme and sound

- You don't need a perfect rhyme everywhere. **Near rhymes** (home / alone, ghost / close) feel natural and modern.
- Keep a **consistent rhyme scheme** inside a section (ABAB or AABB), then break it **once** for emphasis.
- Say the lines **out loud.** If you stumble, the singer will too. Watch for clusters of hard consonants.
- Use **open vowels** (ah, oh, ay) on long, held notes. Closed sounds like "ik" or "ep" are hard to hold.

### Emotion without cliché

- Look at each line and ask: **would anyone else have written this?** If yes, swap the abstract word for a specific detail from your life.
- Prefer the **unexpected true detail** to the expected big word.
- Write the **ugly first draft** without editing, then cut. Cutting is where the lyric appears.

## Part 3: Putting words and melody together

1. **Chords and groove first** (the riff or progression).
2. **Hum melody** over it with nonsense syllables. Record every pass.
3. **Pick the best phrases** and mark them as the hook and verse shapes.
4. **Fit words** to the melody: stresses on strong beats, important words on long notes.
5. **Rewrite** words that fight the rhythm; keep the melody when it works.
6. **Sing it a day later.** Anything you can't remember cold is probably not the hook.

## Part 4: Habits that make you faster

- **Voice-memo everything.** Melodies come at bad times (the car, the shower, while carrying Cyrus). Record them in the moment.
- **One notebook or one note** for lyric fragments. Don't scatter them.
- Set a **short timer** (15 to 25 minutes) for first drafts. Finish a rough song instead of polishing an unfinished one.
- **Write a bad version first.** A finished weak song teaches more than half of a perfect one.
- **Finish before you judge.** Don't edit while you're writing.
- **Steal structures, not words.** Study how a song you love builds its sections (where the hook lands, how the bridge turns) and reuse the shape with your own subject.

## Quick checklist (before a song is "done")

- [ ] I can say what it's about in one sentence
- [ ] The chorus works as a stand-alone line
- [ ] The title or hook appears in the chorus
- [ ] Verse 2 adds something new
- [ ] The bridge changes the meaning or the feeling
- [ ] Every line reads aloud without stumbling
- [ ] The melody is higher and wider in the chorus than the verse
- [ ] I remembered the hook the next day without the recording
